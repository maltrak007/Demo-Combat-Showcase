Case Study 01 — From a Fake Block to Two Real Playstyles

Problem. The original defensive mechanic was a single-button animation: press once, play a fixed montage that disabled player input for its duration, and any hit landing during that window was negated. It looked like blocking but behaved like a scripted invincibility frame — no hold, no stance, no player agency once triggered, and no room for a timing-based mechanic on top of it.

Constraint. The animation set and input scheme were fixed, and combat had to stay readable at speed with multiple enemies on screen.

Decision. Rebuilt it as two layers serving two playstyles rather than one mechanic with a bonus attached. The base layer is a genuine held stance: the player holds the input and stays in block for as long as they hold it, spending stamina per hit absorbed. That's the defensive path — safe, resource-costly, and rewarded when the enemy's guard eventually opens and the player gets a clean opening to attack.

The parry sits on top as a distinct, riskier path rather than a strict upgrade. A visual indicator on the enemy's attack tells the player when to time the parry, but the window isn't placed at the very start of the animation — it's offset, so the player has to anticipate the hit rather than just react to an already-obvious tell. Landing it triggers a heavy counterattack and a window to reposition or open a combo. Missing it means eating a full enemy combo's worth of stamina drain, since the player committed to the parry instead of holding a safe block.

Why two paths instead of one mechanic. An aggressive player who only ever blocks defensively never gets punished for playing safe, but they also never get the payoff moment. A player chasing parries takes on real risk — a whiffed parry costs far more stamina than a held block would have. Structuring it this way meant neither playstyle makes the other one redundant: block-and-wait is always viable, parry-for-reward is always available, and the choice between them is a genuine tactical decision rather than a strictly-better option.

Outcome. The defensive layer went from a scripted animation with no player agency to two distinct, economically balanced paths through the same encounter. The broader lesson: when a mechanic "looks like" a genre standard but is implemented as something else — here, a canned invulnerability animation standing in for blocking — players read the mismatch immediately, even before balance is touched. And once the base mechanic is honest, a higher-risk variant can sit on top of it as a real choice rather than as the only correct way to play.
