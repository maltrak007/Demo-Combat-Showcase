Case Study 02 — One Behavior Tree, Many Skill Levels

Problem. Enemies were a fixed set of numbers baked into behavior tree nodes. A strike count lived on one node, a walk speed on another, a wander width on a third, and nothing decided how many enemies attacked the player at the same time. With three enemies on screen that meant three of them swinging at once, from the same side, on the same beat. Making a novice and a master meant copying the tree and editing the copy, and adding a pistol would have meant copying it again. The combat was readable only while the enemy count stayed low, and the difficulty could only be changed by rebuilding behavior.

Constraint. The game is a side-scroller with at most three enemies on screen: two melee fighters, one on each side of the player, and possibly one shooter on the same plane or slightly above. The moveset system was already data-driven, with each weapon owning an asset and a table of strikes, and it had to stay that way. The ranged weapons would have no combos, only a shot with an animation, and the player had to be able to use them too.

Decision. The work was split into three layers that each answer one question. The first answers who may attack: a director hands out attack tokens, one for each side of the player and one for a shooter, and an enemy without a token waits at a distance and repositions. One token is one sequence of strikes or one burst of shots. The enemy gives the token back when the sequence ends, and only then does its cooldown start, so a slow master never sits on a slot while the other two enemies stand idle. The director also reads the player's recent losses. When the player is hurting it eases the number of attackers down for a while, and when the player is steady it builds the pressure back up.

The second layer answers how well an enemy attacks, and it is one table, not one tree per skill level. A row is keyed by a weapon tag and a mastery tag, and an empty key matches anything, so a row for novices with any weapon and a row for the pistol at every skill level can sit beside an exact pistol-novice row, and the most specific match wins. The archetype is a small modifier on top that scales cooldowns, speed and reload time. Each enemy resolves its row once, caches the result, and rebuilds it on its own when its carried weapon changes. The tree reads that result and never touches the table. The table holds parameters only: how many strikes in a sequence, how long the wait before the next token, how long the telegraph before a shot, how slow the reload, how wide the band the shooter tries to stand in. Which strikes exist stays in the weapon's moveset table, so the two never describe the same thing.

The third layer answers how the player reads a ranged enemy. A shooter does not fire at a random moment the player cannot see coming. It raises its aim for a random time inside a range from its row, and the indicator over its head animates for exactly that time, so the shot lands on the last frame of the animation. The shot itself leaves on a notify inside the montage. A shooter that is hit during the wind-up loses the shot and the indicator, and a reload is the opening a slow mastery gives away, since a novice reloads with the same montage played slower. The shot runs through the same hit resolution as a swing, so a held guard blocks it, damage and stamina work as they do for melee, and a roll lets it pass. A bullet is the one thing a parry-timed guard cannot parry, so it counts as a block.

Why this split. A single value for how aggressive an enemy is would have mixed three different problems: how many attack, how well each attacks, and how fair each attack looks to the player. Kept apart, each can be tuned without breaking the others. The master can have a short cooldown and long combos without ever raising the number of simultaneous attackers, because the director still caps that. The novice can be slow to fire and slow to reload without touching the tree at all.

Setup. Create the two tables from the behavior row and the archetype row, and assign them in Project Settings under Combat Director. Add one row for each weapon and mastery that needs its own numbers, plus a catch-all row with both keys empty so nothing falls back to code defaults. Give each ranged weapon asset a muzzle socket, a range, a magazine size, a reload montage, and a shot row that names an entry in its moveset table. Put a notify tagged Event.Combat.Fire on the shot montage at the frame the bullet should leave. Grant the ranged attack ability to the player and to every shooter. Set the mastery and archetype tags on each enemy. On the behavior tree, put the token service on the combat branch, split the attack branches with the token decorator by melee and ranged, finish each attack sequence with the release task and add the same release task as its fallback sibling, and put the reload task in the branch the enemy runs without a token. In the stat widget blueprint, play the indicator animation from the telegraph event using the duration it passes, and hide it on the end event.

Tuning. The director's phases are the first thing to feel out: how many tokens each phase allows, how long the build-up lasts before full pressure, and how much of the player's health bar a hit has to cost before the director backs off. Then the table. The strike range and the sequence cooldown set the rhythm of a melee fighter, and the pair that matters most is how long a master waits compared with a novice. The fire delay range sets how fair a shooter feels, and its lower end must still be longer than the player's reaction plus the indicator's own blend in. The reload scale sets how big the opening is. The preferred range band decides how much a shooter wanders, and a wide band reads as a sloppy one. Change one number at a time, with the debug view of the director on, so the held tokens and the current phase are visible while it is played.

Outcome. The attack system now has one place that decides how many enemies attack, one place that decides how well they do it, and one signal that tells the player when a shot is coming. A new weapon needs an asset and a row, a new skill level needs a row, and neither needs a new tree. The numbers in the first pass are starting values taken from the intended feel, a novice with short two-strike sequences and a five second wait against a master with longer sequences and a four second wait, and they still have to be tuned in play. The broader lesson: when a difficulty setting is baked into the structure of the AI, every new level of skill multiplies the amount of AI to maintain. When it lives in data that a single tree reads, difficulty becomes a thing that can be tuned.
